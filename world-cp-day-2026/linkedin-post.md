Long before I became a physiotherapist, I carried a child with cerebral palsy. Every morning. 8 a.m.

I was a young boy.

I lifted a small body that could not hold itself up. I wiped drool from a little chin. I watched, always watched, for the next seizure. My heart lived in my throat.

For years, I saw only what that child could not do. No sitting. No walking. No turning of the neck. I measured that child against every other child I knew, and every comparison left me emptier.

Here is what I missed.

The smiles. The laughter. Eyes that followed me across the room. The stillness that came at the sound of a familiar voice. The day a tiny hand stretched, slowly and stubbornly, towards a toy.

That child spoke to me all along. I had not yet learned to listen.

Today, 6 October, is World Cerebral Palsy Day.

I now work with children with cerebral palsy and their families. I do not come to this as a visiting therapist who goes home at the end of the day. I carried, sweated, feared and hoped for years. I know the questions parents ask at night.

I still see the trap I once fell into. Our case notes list missed milestones and little else. Some schools see the diagnosis before they see the pupil. Some neighbours stare instead of saying hello.

Children with cerebral palsy are persons first. They are not a list of delays.

I have seen people with cerebral palsy finish school and earn degrees. I have seen them make their own decisions and change the policies of institutions. Some drive cars. Some fly planes. Not every child will walk that exact road, and that is fine. Every child deserves a road built for them.

I used to ask, "What can this child not do?"
Now I ask, "What world are we building around this child?"

That question belongs to all of us.

→ Therapists: do our goals start with what the child and family want?
→ Teachers: can this child get into the classroom, and take part once inside?
→ Faith leaders: does this family feel welcome at worship?
→ Employers and policymakers: will there be a place for the adult this child becomes?

I often ask parents to try something with me. Each day, catch one thing your child can do. A smile. A glance. A reach. A sound.

I am asking you the same.

If you know a child with cerebral palsy, tell me one thing they CAN do that made you smile. Let us fill this feed with those moments.

To every parent reading this at 2 a.m.: your child is more than a diagnosis. You are more than a carer. 💚
